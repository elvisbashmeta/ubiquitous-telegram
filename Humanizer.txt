Humanizer
Purpose: Strip AI tells out of any text, and keep them out of new text.
Trigger: "humanize this," or apply by default if the skill is loaded.
Applies to: prose. Not code, not tables of specs, not a list of steps someone has to follow.
The one rule underneath all the others
Write the thing. Don't perform the writing.
Most AI tells come from the model showing you it's working: announcing the point before making it, summarizing after making it, adding a clause about why the point matters. A person who has something to say just says it.
What to catch
Fake significance
"stands as," "is a testament to," "played a pivotal role," "cemented its place," "left an indelible mark," "underscores," "reflects broader trends," "symbolizes," "serves as a reminder"
Any sentence claiming importance the surrounding facts don't earn
Notability padding
Listing outlets, awards, or "features" to prove a subject matters
"gained recognition," "has been profiled in," "maintains an active social media presence," "featured in"
Vague association
"connected with," "associated with," "in connection with," "tied to," "linked to" with no stated mechanism
If you can't say how two things relate, don't imply they do
Source theater
"according to his personal website," "is listed in," "available upon request," "not publicly accessible"
If the source can't be checked, drop the claim
Orphan "-ing" clauses
Sentence tails that start with highlighting, reflecting, underscoring, emphasizing, showcasing, ensuring, fostering, demonstrating, marking
These sound like analysis and contain none. Cut the clause, or replace it with a fact.
Promotional tone
"vibrant," "rich," "renowned," "nestled," "boasts," "state-of-the-art," "seamless," "world-class," "cutting-edge"
Read it back as if a competitor wrote it about themselves. If it sounds like a brochure, rewrite.
AI vocabulary
delve, tapestry, landscape, realm, pivotal, crucial, multifaceted, intricate, meticulous, robust, leverage, harness, navigate (figurative), testament, foster, myriad, plethora, embark, unlock, ever-evolving, game-changer
One of these in a long piece isn't a crime. A cluster is a fingerprint.
Negative parallelism
"not only X but also Y," "not just X, it's Y," "X isn't about A, it's about B," "less about X and more about Y"
It's a rhythm crutch. Say the true half and stop.
Reflexive triples
Three adjectives, three examples, three clauses, because three sounds complete
Ask whether the third item earned its place. Sometimes two is right. Sometimes five.
Vague attribution
"some say," "critics argue," "observers note," "many believe," "experts agree," "it's widely considered"
Name the person or cut the sentence
Lecturing
"It's important to note that," "It's worth remembering," "This matters because," "Keep in mind that"
Also: explaining to the reader why a fact is interesting instead of letting the fact be interesting
Hedge stacking
"may potentially," "could arguably," "in some cases it might"
Pick your actual confidence level and state it once
Punctuation tics
Em dashes used for rhythm rather than grammar
Semicolons doing work a period should do
Ellipses trailing off
Symmetry addiction
Every sentence roughly the same length
Every paragraph the same three-sentence shape
Paragraphs that open with a topic sentence, develop, and close with a bow
Formatting tells
Markdown syntax in a plain-text context
Bullets where a paragraph would read better
Bold scattered over key phrases for no reason
"Term: explanation" scaffolding for things that aren't definitions
Headers on two-sentence sections
Structural tells
An intro that restates the prompt, and a conclusion that restates the intro
Both-sidesing when the writer clearly has a view
"Let's dive in," "Here's the thing," "At the end of the day," "In today's world" as transitions
What to do instead
Cut the tail. If a sentence ends in an "-ing" clause, delete the clause. Nine times out of ten the sentence is stronger.
Name the actor. Who did what to whom. Passive voice hides agency; use it only when the agent genuinely doesn't matter.
Swap abstraction for specifics. A number, a name, a date, a concrete noun. "Significant growth" becomes "grew 40% in two years."
Check the source before making the claim. No source, no sentence.
Vary length hard. Follow a 30-word sentence with a 4-word one. Let short sentences land.
Allow asymmetry. Two examples. Or five. Whatever the material actually supports.
State, don't announce. Cut "Here's why this matters" and give the reason.
Prefer "is" to "serves as." Prefer "has" to "boasts." Prefer "used" to "leveraged."
Delete your first and last sentence. In most drafts they're framing, and the real piece starts at sentence two.
Contractions are fine. So is starting with "But" or "And." So is a fragment.
Voice defaults
Contractions, unless the context is formal
Plain words over Latinate ones: "use" not "utilize," "help" not "facilitate," "show" not "demonstrate"
Say "I don't know" instead of hedging into mush
Admit the limits of your own claim in one clause, not three
Don't summarize what you just said
One idea per sentence, mostly
Self-check before sending
Read-aloud test. Would a person say this sentence out loud? If not, rewrite.
First/last test. Cover the first and last sentence. Does the piece still work? If yes, leave them covered.
Source test. For every claim, can you name where it comes from? If not, cut it.
Triple test. Count your lists of three. If more than one, break them up.
Vocabulary test. Scan for the AI vocabulary list. More than one hit, rewrite those sentences.
Symmetry test. Look at sentence lengths. If they're all within a few words of each other, break the pattern on purpose.
Exceptions
Lists are correct for steps, specs, comparisons, and anything a reader will scan rather than read. Structure is correct when it does work for the reader. This skill targets prose that's pretending to be thought, not formatting that's actually helping.

Part 2: Scoring rubric
Score each cluster 1–10. 10 means no trace of the tell. 1 means the draft is made of it. Tally at the end.
Cluster 1 — Significance inflation
1: Every other sentence claims importance. "Pivotal," "testament," "cemented."
5: A few claims of importance that the facts half-support.
10: The facts are stated. The reader decides what they mean.
How to score fast: Highlight every sentence that says something matters. Count them. More than one per 300 words is a problem.
Cluster 2 — Orphan clauses
1: Most sentences end in an "-ing" phrase.
5: Three or four in a page.
10: None, or the rare one that carries real information ("grew 12%, outpacing every competitor").
Fast check: Search for ", highlighting," ", reflecting," ", underscoring," ", showcasing," ", ensuring." Delete the results and see if anything is lost.
Cluster 3 — Vocabulary
1: Two or more AI words per paragraph.
5: One per paragraph, scattered.
10: None, or a word that happens to be on the list but is genuinely the right word. ("Robust" in a statistics context is fine.)
Fast check: Search the list. Count hits. Divide by word count. Above 1 per 200 words, rewrite.
Cluster 4 — Symmetry
1: Every sentence 15–25 words. Every paragraph three sentences.
5: Some variation, but a steady drumbeat.
10: A four-word sentence sits next to a forty-word one. Paragraphs run 1 to 8 sentences.
Fast check: Count words per sentence. If the standard deviation is small, break the pattern.
Cluster 5 — Hedging and vagueness
1: "Some say," "may potentially," "it could be argued."
5: Occasional vagueness where the writer clearly knows the answer.
10: Claims attributed to named sources, confidence stated once.
Fast check: Search for "some," "many," "experts," "critics," "observers," "arguably," "potentially." Each hit is a sentence that needs a name or a cut.
Cluster 6 — Structure
1: Intro restates the prompt. Conclusion restates the intro. Headers on tiny sections.
5: Conventional but functional shape.
10: Starts where the interesting part starts. Ends when the point is made.
Fast check: Read only the first and last paragraph. If they say the same thing, cut one.
Cluster 7 — Formatting
1: Bold scattered everywhere, bullets for prose, headers every two sentences.
5: Formatting that's defensible but heavy.
10: Formatting only where the reader will scan.
Fast check: Remove all bold. Does anything get harder to read? If not, leave it removed.
Tally
Add all seven. Out of 70.
60–70: Ship it.
45–59: Fix the lowest two clusters.
30–44: Rewrite the weak sections rather than patching.
Below 30: Start over. Patching won't save it.
One more thing about scoring: the clusters aren't equally weighted in how they read. Significance inflation and orphan clauses are the loudest tells. If you're short on time, fix those two first.
Part 3: Stress-test passages
Each passage below contains one tell, deliberately. Cover the label, read the passage, name the problem, then rewrite it. Answers are underneath, flipped.
Passage 1
The bridge opened in 1937 and quickly became a symbol of the city's resilience, standing as a testament to the ingenuity of its engineers and reflecting the broader optimism of the era.
Tell: significance inflation plus two stacked orphan clauses.
Rewrite: The bridge opened in 1937. Its engineers solved the span problem by [specific method]. (The "symbol of resilience" claim needs a source or a cut.)
Passage 2
Critics have argued that the policy was flawed, and some observers note that its effects may potentially have been uneven across different regions.
Tell: vague attribution stacked on hedge stacking.
Rewrite: [Named economist] found the policy's effects were uneven across regions. (Or: cut the sentence. If nobody can be named, nobody said it.)
Passage 3
The company boasts a vibrant, world-class team and maintains an active presence across multiple social platforms.
Tell: promotional tone plus notability padding. This sentence exists to make the subject sound important and says nothing.
Rewrite: The company employs 40 people. It posts on LinkedIn and X. (If the social presence matters, say what it's used for.)
Passage 4
Not only did the reform reduce processing times, but it also improved worker satisfaction, demonstrating the value of thoughtful policy design.
Tell: negative parallelism, a reflexive triple, and an orphan clause in one sentence.
Rewrite: Processing times fell 30%. Worker satisfaction scores rose. (Let the reader draw the conclusion. Or state it plainly: "Both improvements suggest the design mattered.")
Passage 5
The city is nestled in a lush valley, offering visitors a rich tapestry of cultural experiences and a myriad of dining options.
Tell: the full AI vocabulary cluster. Four tells in twenty words.
Rewrite: The city sits in a valley. Its restaurant scene runs from [specific cuisine] to [specific cuisine].
Passage 6
It's important to note that these findings are preliminary and should be interpreted with caution, as further research is needed.
Tell: lecturing disclaimer. The reader knows preliminary findings are preliminary.
Rewrite: The sample was 30 people. (State the limitation as a fact. Skip the instruction about how to feel about it.)
Passage 7
In today's fast-paced world, businesses must leverage cutting-edge technology to navigate an ever-evolving landscape and unlock their full potential.
Tell: every generic-phrase bingo card in one sentence. This says nothing at all.
Rewrite: Delete it. There's nothing here to save. Start with the actual claim.
How to use these
Read one aloud, spot the tell, rewrite in your own words. Then compare to the answer. If your rewrite and mine differ but both pass the read-aloud test, yours is fine. The point isn't matching me, it's building the reflex.
Once you can catch all seven in under a minute, you're ready for the real test: run your own draft through the rubric, score it honestly, and fix the lowest cluster.
